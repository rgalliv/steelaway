# Data Handling

The README's fifth best practice — *never share proprietary bid numbers, client
contracts, or confidential pricing* — deserves more than one line, because these
templates ask you to paste contracts and bids by design.

## The tension

The templates work because they get complete documents. Partial input produces
partial analysis. But those documents are often the most sensitive things your
company holds. Both facts are true and the resolution is judgment, not a rule.

## Before pasting, ask

**Is it under an NDA or confidentiality clause?** Many subcontracts and owner
agreements restrict disclosure to third parties. Whether an AI service counts is
a question for counsel, and the answer may depend on the service tier and its
data terms. Ask once, for your company, rather than per document.

**Whose information is it?** Your own estimate is yours to handle. A competitor's
sealed bid, an owner's confidential budget, or another sub's pricing shared for a
limited purpose is not.

**Does it contain personal information?** Crew rosters, injury records, and
payroll carry obligations beyond commercial confidentiality.

**Would disclosure harm someone?** Not just you. A vendor's pricing, disclosed,
harms the vendor.

## Practical reductions

Most templates need *structure*, not identity:

- **Redact names.** "GC-A" and "Vendor B" analyze the same as real names in a
  bid comparison.
- **Scale the numbers.** Consistently scaled pricing preserves the ratios that
  drive comparison.
- **Excerpt by section.** A subcontract risk review needs the payment, indemnity,
  changes, and disputes articles — not the whole book, on a first pass.
- **Strip identifiers.** Project addresses, permit numbers, and owner names rarely
  affect the analysis.

Redaction costs a few minutes and removes most of the question.

## What cannot be reduced

Some analysis genuinely requires the real document — exact clause language for a
risk review, exact dates for a notice analysis. When it does, that is a decision
about which document goes where, and it should be made deliberately and once,
with counsel's input, rather than repeatedly under deadline.

## Check your own terms

Data handling, retention, and training use differ by product and plan. Check
current terms for the account you are using rather than relying on what a
colleague remembers. If your company has an AI use policy, it governs — and if it
does not have one, this is a good reason to write one before the library gets
wide use.
